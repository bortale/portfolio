HR · Operations · L&D · Case Study 03

Under Pressure, From Scratch:
Building a Seasonal Business System

This case is about a business I founded, funded, and ran. I took a loan, built the operation from zero, and made decisions where every mistake had immediate financial consequences. The business reached operational profitability and was discontinued in 2025 due to changes in residency status — not operational or financial failure.
Role
Founder & Operations Lead
Context
Seasonal food service, Gotland, Sweden
Duration
3 seasons · 2023–2025
Scale
11 → 24 employees · 1 → 3 units
The Real Context

This wasn't a consulting project. It was my money.

What the numbers don't show

I took a loan to start this business. I was operating on an island with a limited labour market, extreme weather dependency, and seasonal demand that could disappear overnight. There was no safety net. Every recruitment mistake, every inventory loss, every bad hire — came directly out of cash flow I couldn't afford to lose.

The systems I built weren't designed because I wanted to demonstrate HR best practices. They were designed because without them, the business wouldn't survive. That's a different kind of pressure than consulting work — and it produces a different kind of learning.

This context matters because it explains why the decisions were made the way they were — conservatively, incrementally, always with one eye on cash flow — and why the results, while not dramatic on paper, represent real operational progress under real constraints.

01 — Starting Point

What didn't exist at the start

Before Season 1, there was no HR infrastructure, no documented processes, no recruitment strategy, and no operational framework. Knowledge lived in people's heads. Every season started from scratch.

💡 The constraint wasn't resources — it was the absence of any system. Problems were solved one by one, reactively, at the cost of time and money the business couldn't afford.

The pre-season period of 2023 was spent diagnosing: where do things break, what costs the most, where does dependency on individuals create risk?

02 — Three Seasons

Built one season at a time

Season 1 · 2023 — Build the Foundation

Structure before the season starts

The first objective was simple: stop starting from zero every year. Build something that holds.

  • Designed role-based shift structure — cash, prep, service — with clear ownership at each position
  • Rebuilt recruitment from scratch: partnered with local schools, Arbetsförmedlingen, community radio; became an approved praktik placement site
  • Created onboarding framework: role instructions, visual SOPs, shift checklists — designed for fast independent execution
  • Introduced cost screening and procurement controls — every purchase evaluated against cash-flow impact
  • All positions filled before season launch — first time this happened

Key result: Launch costs reduced by 79% against planned budget — without compromising operational readiness. This was the single most important early signal that the system approach was working.

Season 2 · 2024 — Test and Improve

Does the system hold under real pressure?

Season 2 was not about growth. It was about finding out whether what was built actually worked when things got hard — weather drops, inexperienced staff, peak demand, equipment failures.

  • Deepened role specialisation to increase throughput without adding headcount
  • Introduced short daily briefings — low time cost, significant alignment value
  • Built a frontline feedback loop: employees identified bottlenecks, I acted on them
  • Redesigned service flow: QR payments, pre-prepared high-margin items (e.g. pre-packaged salmon salads), simplified customer journey
  • Introduced weather-based demand planning to reduce waste on low-traffic days
  • Maintained purchasing discipline throughout — procurement costs stayed within target

Key result: The business reached operational profitability for the first time. Modest — but real. More importantly, the system held. That was the validation needed to consider growth in Season 3.

Season 3 · 2025 — Controlled Growth

Use the system as a platform

Every decision in Season 3 was based on data from the previous two seasons. No guessing. No optimism. Growth only where the system could absorb it.

  • Expanded from 1 to 3 operational units — 4th was planned but not launched
  • Workforce grew from 11 to 24 employees without proportional increase in management load
  • Praktik-to-hire pipeline matured: 29% of workforce (7 of 24) came through the internship channel
  • Internal leadership pathway introduced — reliable employees progressed into shift and unit manager roles based on demonstrated performance
  • Orders per hour increased from 4.0 to 6.8 — approximately 70% productivity improvement
  • Purchasing discipline maintained and improved through menu optimisation and tighter inventory management

Key result: Profitability improved consistently season over season. The business became less dependent on individuals and more dependent on systems — which was the original objective.

03 — The L&D Layer

Learning had to happen inside the work

A seasonal food service business cannot run training days. There is no time, no budget, and no tolerance for people who aren't operational from day one. Learning had to be built into the workflow — or it didn't exist.

→

Onboarding as a learning system

SOPs and checklists were designed as learning tools — not compliance documents. The goal was that a new employee could execute their role independently within the first shift, without needing a manager to answer every question.

→

Operational decisions as learning data

The salmon salad example: I noticed throughput was limited by order preparation time during peaks. The solution wasn't "work faster" — it was redesigning what was prepared when. Pre-packaged high-margin items reduced wait time and increased revenue per hour. This is performance consulting applied to a food truck.

→

Frontline feedback as continuous improvement

Employees were treated as the primary source of operational intelligence. Their observations about friction, bottlenecks, and customer behaviour were collected, acted on, and fed back into process design. Classic action learning loop — in a context where it actually had to work.

→

Internal talent pipeline as structured progression

The praktik model created a real learning pathway: internship → paid role → shift manager → unit manager. Progression was competency-based — earned through demonstrated performance under pressure, not seniority. By Season 3, 29% of the workforce came through this channel.

05 — Reflection

What this case actually taught me

"Sustainable growth occurs when people systems, operational systems, and financial systems evolve together. Growth must be absorbed by the system — not compensated for through individual effort."

The business was discontinued in 2025 due to changes in my residency status in Sweden — not as a result of operational or financial performance. By that point, the system was working. Profitability had improved every season. The 4th unit was planned.

What I take from this into L&D work is not the food service experience — it's the discipline of building under constraint. When mistakes cost real money and you can't afford to wait for the next training cycle, you learn very quickly what actually changes behaviour and what doesn't.

SOPs that nobody reads don't work. Checklists embedded in the workflow do. Feedback that goes nowhere kills engagement. Feedback that visibly changes something creates ownership. These aren't L&D theories to me — they're things I tested with my own money.
